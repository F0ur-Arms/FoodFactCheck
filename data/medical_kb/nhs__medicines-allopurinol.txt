Allopurinol
Allopurinol is a medicine mainly used to treat gout. It's only available with a prescription.
What allopurinol is for
Allopurinol helps reduce the amount of uric acid in your body. Uric acid is a waste product that can cause problems if levels get too high.
It can be used to:
prevent gout attacks
prevent some types of kidney stones
prevent problems caused by uric acid build-up during some types of cancer treatment
How to take allopurinol
Allopurinol comes as tablets or as a liquid (oral suspension) that you swallow.
You usually need to take it once a day, but if you're taking a higher dose you may need to take it up to 3 times a day. Your doctor will explain your dose to you.
How long you need to take it for depends on why you're taking it. If you're taking it for gout or kidney stones, you may need to take it long term.
When taking allopurinol:
swallow tablets whole with a glass of water – you can break them in half if your doctor asks you to take a smaller dose
use the syringe, cup or spoon that comes with the liquid medicine to help you measure the correct dose
it's best to take allopurinol with food
drink plenty of fluids to stay hydrated
Always check the packet or leaflet that comes with your medicine. Ask a pharmacist or doctor for advice if you're not sure how to take it or have any problems taking it.
If you have gout, you may be more likely to get gout attacks when you first start taking allopurinol. Your doctor will explain this to you and may give you other medicine to help.
Do not stop taking allopurinol unless your doctor tells you to.
If you miss a dose of allopurinol
If you forget to take a dose of allopurinol, take it as soon as you remember unless it's nearly time for your next dose.
If it's nearly time for your next dose, skip the dose you missed and take your next dose at the usual time.
Do not take 2 doses to make up for a missed dose.
If you take too much allopurinol
Taking an extra dose of allopurinol is unlikely to cause problems, but it's important to get medical advice if you take too much as it can sometimes be dangerous.
Urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist as soon as possible if:
you've taken more than your prescribed dose of allopurinol
If you're unable to speak to a pharmacist, or you took too much of your medicine on purpose, call NHS 111.

Side effects of allopurinol
Like all medicines, allopurinol can cause side effects, although not everyone gets them.
Possible side effects of allopurinol include:
a rash or other skin reactions – stop taking allopurinol and speak to your doctor straight away if this happens
feeling sick (nausea) or being sick (vomiting)
diarrhoea
Allopurinol will not usually affect your ability to drive, ride a bike or use machinery, but if you feel drowsy, dizzy, sleepy or have problems with your coordination after taking it wait until you no longer have until these side effects.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist or doctor if:
you have any side effects that bother you or do not go away
They may be able to give you advice about how you can reduce the side effects.

Serious side effects
Allopurinol can sometimes cause serious side effects, although most of them are rare.
Possible serious side effects of allopurinol include:
a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) – causing symptoms such as a swollen throat or tongue, a raised, itchy rash or difficulty breathing
a severe skin reaction (Stevens-Johnson syndrome) – causing flu-like symptoms, a rash, blisters and sores
meningitis – causing symptoms such as a stiff neck, feeling sick, a severe headache and feeling confused
a serious problem with your white blood cells – causing symptoms such as bruising easily, feeling very tired, a sudden sore throat or a high temperature (you may feel hot, cold or shivery)
Immediate action required: Call 999 if:
you're taking allopurinol and think you might be having anaphylaxis, symptoms of Stevens-Johnson syndrome or symptoms of meningitis
If you think you're having any other serious side effect, call NHS 111.

Who can and cannot take allopurinol
Most adults can take allopurinol. It's sometimes prescribed for children if they have certain types of cancer.
But allopurinol is not suitable for everyone. For example, it may not be suitable if:
you've had an allergic reaction or other serious side effect from allopurinol before
you're of Han Chinese, Thai, Korean, African or Indian ethnic origin – people from these ethnic origins may be more likely to have a severe reaction to allopurinol
you have thyroid, kidney or liver problems
Before taking allopurinol, tell your doctor if you have any other medical conditions.
Breastfeeding and allopurinol
Speak to your doctor if you're breastfeeding.
Allopurinol can pass into breast milk. It's not known to be harmful for your baby, but there is limited evidence about its effects.
Your doctor will speak to you about the benefits and risks of taking it.
It's rare for breastfed babies to get any side effects from medicine you're taking, but speak to a healthcare professional if you have any concerns about your baby.
Pregnancy and allopurinol
Tell your doctor if you're pregnant or planning a pregnancy while taking allopurinol. They may recommend switching to a different medicine.
Allopurinol is not usually used during pregnancy because it may affect how your baby develops. But a doctor may recommend it if the benefits outweigh the risks.
Find out more about taking allopurinol during pregnancy on the Bumps (Best Use of Medicines in Pregnancy) website
Taking allopurinol with other medicines, food and drink
There are some medicines, remedies and supplements that may not mix well with allopurinol.
This includes:
aspirin, or medicines to help prevent blood clots (anticoagulants) such as warfarin
theophylline, a medicine to treat asthma
some medicines for high blood pressure or heart problems, such as ACE inhibitors or medicines to make you pee more (diuretics)
some antibiotics, such as amoxicillin
some medicines that lower your immune system (immunosuppressants), such as azathioprine
Before taking allopurinol, tell your doctor if you're taking any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.
Food, drink and alcohol with allopurinol
You can eat and drink as normal while taking allopurinol.
