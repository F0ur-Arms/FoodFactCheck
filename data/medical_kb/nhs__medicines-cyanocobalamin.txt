Cyanocobalamin
Cyanocobalamin is a vitamin that's taken as a medicine or supplement to prevent and treat vitamin B12 deficiency. It's available on prescription and you can buy cyanocobalamin supplements from shops or pharmacies.
What cyanocobalamin is for
Cyanocobalamin is a man-made (artificial) version of vitamin that's used to treat and prevent vitamin B12 deficiency, which happens when you have low levels of this vitamin in your body.
Your body needs vitamin B12 to make red blood cells. Red blood cells carry oxygen around the body.
You can get vitamin B12 from your food or supplements, but sometimes you might not be able to get enough. This can happen if you have a condition that affects how your body absorbs vitamin B12, you have a vegan diet, or you do not eat much meat or dairy products.
If you have a vitamin B12 deficiency, your doctor may prescribe hydroxocobalamin injections, another type of vitamin B12.
How to take cyanocobalamin
Cyanocobalamin usually comes as tablets that you swallow. How much you need to take and for how long depends on why you’re taking it.
When taking cyanocobalamin:
swallow tablets whole with a glass of water
take tablets between meals
Always check the packet or leaflet that comes with your medicine. Ask a pharmacist or doctor for advice if you're not sure how to use it or have any problems using it.
If you miss a dose of cyanocobalamin
If you forget to take a dose, take it as soon as you remember, unless it's nearly time for your next dose.
If it's nearly time for your next dose, skip the dose you missed and take your next dose at the usual time.
If you take too much cyanocobalamin
Taking an extra dose of cyanocobalamin is unlikely to cause problems, but it's important to get medical advice if you take too much as it can be dangerous.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist if:
you've taken more cyanocobalamin than it says on the packet, leaflet or prescription and it's causing any symptoms
If you're unable to speak to a pharmacist, or you took too much of your medicine on purpose, call NHS 111 for advice.

If you're told to go to A&E, do not drive. Ask someone to drive you or call 999 and ask for an ambulance.
Bring the cyanocobalamin packet or leaflet inside it, any remaining medicine and any other medicines you take.
Side effects of cyanocobalamin
Like all medicines, cyanocobalamin can cause side effects, although not everyone gets them.
Side effects can include spots or rashes that look similar to acne.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist or doctor if:
you have any side effects that bother you or do not go away
They may be able to give you advice about how you can reduce the side effects. Keep taking your medicine unless you’re advised to stop.

Serious side effects
Cyanocobalamin can sometimes cause serious side effects, although most of them are rare.
Possible serious side effects of cyanocobalamin include:
an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) – causing symptoms such as a swollen throat or tongue, a raised, itchy rash or difficulty breathing
skin reactions – causing symptoms such a rash with blisters, feeling faint, wheezing or difficulty breathing
Immediate action required: Call 999 if:
you're taking cyanocobalamin and think you might be having anaphylaxis
If you think you're having any other serious side effect, call NHS 111.
More about side effects
These are not all the side effects of cyanocobalamin.
For a full list, see the leaflet that comes with your medicine or search for your medicine leaflet on the electronic medicines compendium website.
You can report any side effects you get on the Yellow Card website
Who can and cannot take cyanocobalamin
Most adults and children can take cyanocobalamin.
But cyanocobalamin is not suitable for everyone. For example, it may not be suitable if:
you've had an allergic reaction or other serious side effect from cyanocobalamin or another vitamin B12
you're allergic to cobalt
Before taking cyanocobalamin, tell your doctor if you have any medical conditions.
Breastfeeding and cyanocobalamin
You may be able to take cyanocobalamin while you're breastfeeding, but check with a pharmacist or doctor first.
It's rare for breastfed babies to get any side effects from medicine you're taking, but speak to a healthcare professional if you have any concerns about your baby or call NHS 111.
Pregnancy and cyanocobalamin
You can usually take cyanocobalamin if you're pregnant, but check with your pharmacists or doctor first.
Find out more about taking cyanocobalamin during pregnancy on the Bumps (Best Use of Medicines in Pregnancy) website
Taking cyanocobalamin with other medicines, food and drink
There are some medicines, remedies and supplements that may not mix well with cyanocobalamin.
This includes:
H2 blockers like ranitidine, famotidine or nizatidine
proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole
Before taking cyanocobalamin, tell your doctor if you're taking any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.
Food, drink and alcohol with cyanocobalamin
You can eat and drink as normal while taking cyanocobalamin.
