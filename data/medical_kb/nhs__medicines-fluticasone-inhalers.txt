Fluticasone inhalers
- Common brands: Flixotide
Fluticasone inhalers are an type of steroid medicine used to treat asthma. They're only available with a prescription.
There’s separate information about fluticasone nasal spray and drops for treating allergic rhinitis.
What fluticasone inhalers are for
Fluticasone inhalers contain a steroid medicine. They are called preventer inhalers because they help prevent asthma attacks.
Preventer inhalers can help:
control asthma symptoms
reduce the chance of having an asthma attack
A preventer inhaler is different to the reliever inhaler that you use during an asthma attack.
There are also combination inhalers, containing fluticasone and another medicine, to treat asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
How to use fluticasone inhalers
Fluticasone comes as an inhaler that you use twice a day. There are different types and strengths.
Always check the packet or leaflet that comes with your medicine. Ask a pharmacist, nurse or doctor for advice if you're not sure how to use it or have any problems taking it.
Your GP, a nurse or pharmacist will show you how to use your inhaler. Sometimes you’ll be given a clear plastic tube called a spacer to attach to the inhaler. This makes it easier to breathe in your medicine
When using fluticasone:
use the inhaler exactly as you’ve been shown
try to use it at the same time each day
rinse you mouth with water after using your inhaler and spit the water out
While using fluticasone, you may be given a steroid alert card.
If a child is prescribed fluticasone, they’ll have their height checked regularly by their doctor. This is because steroids can sometimes affect how children grow.
Do not stop using your fluticasone inhaler suddenly or without talking to your doctor first, even if you feel well. It could make your asthma worse.
Always take the exact dose as prescribed by your GP. If your asthma symptoms are not well controlled and you're having to use your inhalers more often, speak to your GP. They may need to review your asthma medicines.
If you miss a dose of fluticasone
If you forget to use your fluticasone inhaler, skip the missed dose and take your next dose at the usual time.
Do not take 2 doses to make up for a missed dose.
If you take too much fluticasone
Do not worry if you sometimes use more fluticasone than you should. But avoid regularly using too much because it can increase the risk of side effects.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist if:
you've used more fluticasone than it says on the packet, leaflet or prescription and it’s causing symptoms, such as a sore throat or oral thrush

Side effects of fluticasone inhalers
Like all medicines, fluticasone inhalers can cause side effects, although not everyone gets them.
Common side effects
Common side effects of fluticasone inhalers include:
a headache
oral thrush (mouth thrush)
a croaky or hoarse voice
sore throat
Fluticasone inhalers will not usually affect your ability to drive, ride a bike or use machinery.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist or doctor if:
you have any side effects that bother you or do not go away
They may be able to give you advice about how you can reduce the side effects. Keep taking your medicine unless you’re advised to stop.

Serious side effects
Fluticasone inhalers can sometimes cause serious side effects, although most of them are rare.
Possible serious side effects of inhaled fluticasone include:
an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) – which can cause symptoms such as a swollen throat or tongue, a raised, itchy rash or difficulty breathing
sudden difficulty breathing due to restricted airways – use your reliever inhaler if this happens
glaucoma or cataracts – causing problems with your eyes such as blurred vision, double vision or eye pain
adrenal insufficiency – causing symptoms such as vomiting, muscle weakness, loss of appetite, weight loss, mood changes or feeling dizzy, faint or very tired
Immediate action required: Call 999 if:
you're using a fluticasone inhaler and think you might be having anaphylaxis, or you’re having severe difficulty breathing and your reliever inhaler has not helped
If you think you're having any other serious side effect, call NHS 111.
More about side effects
These are not all the side effects of fluticasone inhalers.
For a full list, see the leaflet that comes with your medicine or search for your medicine leaflet on the electronic medicines compendium website.
You can report any side effects you get on the Yellow Card website
Who can and cannot use fluticasone inhalers
Most adults can use fluticasone inhalers.
It's sometimes also prescribed for children aged 4 years or older.
But fluticasone inhalers are not suitable for everyone. For example, they may not be suitable if:
you've had an allergic reaction or other serious side effect from fluticasone or another inhaled steroid
you’ve had treatment for tuberculosis (TB)
Before using fluticasone inhalers, tell your doctor if you have any medical conditions.
Breastfeeding and fluticasone inhalers
You can usually use a fluticasone inhaler while breastfeeding.
It's rare for breastfed babies to get any side effects from medicine you're taking, but speak to a healthcare professional if you have any concerns about your baby.
Pregnancy and fluticasone inhalers
You can usually use fluticasone inhalers if you're pregnant.
It's important that your asthma is controlled, to keep you and your baby safe.
Find out more about using fluticasone inhalers during pregnancy on the Bumps (Best Use of Medicines in Pregnancy) website
Using fluticasone inhalers with other medicines, food and drink
There are some medicines, remedies and supplements that may not mix well with fluticasone inhalers.
This includes:
steroid tablets (corticosteroids)
some antifungal medicines taken as tablets or capsules
ritonavir, an antiviral medicine used to treat HIV
Before using a fluticasone inhaler, tell your doctor if you're taking any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.
Food, drink and alcohol with fluticasone inhalers
You can eat and drink as normal while using a fluticasone inhaler.
