Key points
Cigarette smoking is the number one risk factor for lung cancer.
Radon is another important cause of lung cancer in the United States.
Smoking
Cigarette smoking is the number one risk factor for lung cancer. In the United States, cigarette smoking is linked to about 80% of lung cancer deaths. Using other tobacco products such as cigars or pipes also increases the risk for lung cancer. Tobacco smoke is a toxic mix of more than 7,000 chemicals. Many are poisons. At least 70 are known to cause cancer in people or animals.
People who smoke cigarettes are 15 to 30 times more likely to get lung cancer or die from lung cancer than people who do not smoke. Even smoking a few cigarettes a day or smoking occasionally increases the risk of lung cancer. The more years a person smokes and the more cigarettes smoked each day, the more risk goes up. A person's risk of getting lung cancer goes down after they quit smoking.
Cigarette smoking can cause cancer almost anywhere in the body: the mouth, throat, esophagus, stomach, colon, rectum, liver, pancreas, voice box (larynx), lung, trachea, bronchus, kidney and renal pelvis, urinary bladder, and cervix. Smoking can also cause acute myeloid leukemia.
Smoke from other people's cigarettes, pipes, or cigars (secondhand smoke) also causes lung cancer.

Radon
Indoor radon is another important cause of lung cancer in the United States. Radon is a naturally occurring gas that forms in rocks, soil, and water. It cannot be seen, tasted, or smelled. When radon gets into homes or buildings through cracks or holes, it can get trapped and build up in the air inside. People who live or work in these homes and buildings breathe in high radon levels. Over long periods of time, radon can cause lung cancer.
The risk of lung cancer from radon exposure is higher for people who smoke than for people who don't smoke. But radon can cause lung cancer in people who have never smoked cigarettes. Learn how to test your home for radon and reduce the radon level if it is high.

Other risk factors
Substances at some workplaces
Examples of substances found at some workplaces that increase risk include asbestos, arsenic, diesel exhaust, and some forms of silica and chromium. For many of these substances, the risk of getting lung cancer is even higher for those who smoke. Living in areas with higher levels of air pollution also increases the risk of getting lung cancer.
Personal or family history of lung cancer
If you are a lung cancer survivor, there is a risk that you may develop another lung cancer, especially if you smoke. Your risk of lung cancer may be higher if your parents, brothers or sisters, or children have had lung cancer. This could be true because they also smoke or live or work in the same place where they are exposed to substances that can cause lung cancer. This could also be true because of an inherited genetic mutation.
HIV infection
People with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) have a higher risk of getting lung cancer than those who are not infected.
Radiation therapy to the chest
Cancer survivors who had radiation therapy to the chest are at higher risk of lung cancer.
Beta carotene supplements
Beta carotene is a red-orange pigment found naturally in fruits and vegetables such as carrots, sweet potatoes, and spinach. Eating foods high in beta carotene does not increase risk of lung cancer. However, taking beta carotene supplements (pills) increases the risk of getting lung cancer, especially in people who smoke at least one pack of cigarettes a day. The risk is higher in people who also drink alcohol every day.
Arsenic and radon in well water
Arsenic and radon in drinking water (primarily from private wells) can increase the risk of lung cancer.
