Key points
Stillbirth is more common than people may realize and remains a sad reality for many families.
If you’re pregnant and notice something that feels unusual or worries you, talk to your doctor.
What it is
A stillbirth is the loss of a pregnancy at or after the 20th week of pregnancy. Stillbirth is different from miscarriage. In the United States, a miscarriage is usually defined as the loss of a fetus before the 20th week of pregnancy.
Stillbirth is further classified as early, late, or term:
Early stillbirth: Loss between 20 and 27 weeks of pregnancy.
Late stillbirth: Loss between 28 and 36 weeks of pregnancy.
Term stillbirth: Loss at 37 weeks of pregnancy or later.
Who is at risk
Stillbirth can affect families of all backgrounds. While most pregnancies result in a healthy birth, stillbirth occurs more commonly among certain groups of people, including women who
are 35 years of age or older
are of low socioeconomic status
smoke cigarettes during pregnancy
have certain medical conditions, such as high blood pressure
are pregnant with more than one baby (such as twins or triplets)
have had a stillbirth in the past
Stillbirth rates are higher in some racial or ethnic groups. In 2020, stillbirth occurred among non-Hispanic Black women two times more often than among non-Hispanic White and Asian or Pacific Islander women.12
These differences are not caused by biological or genetic differences between races and ethnicities. Researchers continue to study the many factors that may contribute to causes, including access to quality health care, pre-existing health conditions, and other factors that can affect pregnancy. Learning more about these factors helps improve our understanding of stillbirth and identify opportunities to create programs to prevent them.
Adrian's story
My son Adrian was stillborn due to complications from undiagnosed preeclampsia. Preeclampsia is a condition that can happen after 20 weeks of pregnancy. No one mentioned the risk of stillbirth, and none of my providers seemed concerned about my symptoms. The night before I hit my 41st week of pregnancy, I went to bed with my son actively kicking in my belly. By the time I woke up the following morning, he was dead. I wish I had known stillbirth is as common as it is. I wish I had known that having symptoms of preeclampsia was a big deal. Most of all, I wish more people talked about stillbirth. My son should be here, and he isn't.
What CDC is doing
CDC works to learn more about stillbirth. CDC does this by tracking how often stillbirth occurs and researching possible causes. This information can be used to develop recommendations, policies, and services to help prevent stillbirth. While we continue to learn more about stillbirth, much work remains.
