At a glance
Genetic tests you order yourself and take at home may not give you a clear picture of your risk for hereditary breast, ovarian, and related cancers, especially if cancer runs in your family.
Background
Some people take a genetic test on their own to learn about their risk for certain diseases, including cancer. This type of test, called a direct-to-consumer genetic test, can be taken without seeing a genetic counselor or doctor. It can tell you about your ancestry and genetic traits, including some information about your risk of having a change in your breast cancer (BRCA) genes.
Differences between direct-to-consumer and clinical genetic tests
There are several differences between a direct-to-consumer genetic test and a clinical genetic test given to you by a doctor or genetic counselor.
Clinical genetic tests look for thousands of possible mutations that can increase your cancer risk. The direct-to-consumer genetic test approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) looks for 44 specific BRCA gene mutations.
Clinical genetic tests look at BRCA1, BRCA2, and other genes. The direct-to-consumer genetic test looks only at BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes.
Doctors can make medical decisions based on the results of clinical tests, not direct-to-consumer tests.
Are you worried about your cancer risk?
A direct-to-consumer genetic test may not give you a complete understanding of your cancer risk, especially if cancer runs in your family. A negative result on a direct-to-consumer genetic test does not rule out the possibility that you carry other genetic mutations that increase your risk. If you have a family history of cancer, do not rely on a direct-to-consumer genetic test to learn about your risk.
If you think you may be at higher risk for hereditary cancer, it is best to talk to a doctor or genetic counselor who can assess your risk, interpret your results, and build an action plan.
Steps you can take
Learn about your family's history of cancer.
Tell your doctor about your family history.
Ask your doctor if you are at high risk because of your family history.
If you took a direct-to-consumer genetic test, share your results with your doctor. Your doctor may recommend that you get a clinical genetic test.
Ask your doctor about working with a genetic counselor to assess your genetic risk and build an action plan.
Practice healthy behaviors to lower your risk, like keeping a healthy weight and being physically active.
