Utrogestan
Utrogestan is a medicine mostly used to treat menopause symptoms, usually as part of hormone replacement therapy (HRT). It's only available with a prescription.
What Utrogestan is for
Utrogestan is used to ease perimenopause and menopause symptoms, such as hot flushes and mood changes. It's usually taken with other medicines as part of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) to protect your womb lining.
It's also sometimes used:
during fertility treatment
during pregnancy, if there's a risk you may give birth early
Utrogestan is a type of medicine called a progestogen. It works like the hormone progesterone, which your body makes less of as you go through menopause.
How to take Utrogestan
Utrogestan comes as capsules you swallow (oral capsules) or capsules you put inside your vagina (vaginal capsules).
How much you take and how long you use it for depends on your symptoms.
Your doctor will give you advice on how and when to take Utrogestan, and will usually arrange a follow-up appointment to check how well it's working.
How to take different types of Utrogestan
Oral capsules
When taking Utrogestan oral capsules for menopause symptoms:
take your prescribed dose at bedtime
swallow the capsules whole with a glass of water
do not take the capsules with food
take the capsules at the same time as your oestrogen HRT medicine
Vaginal capsules
When taking Utrogestan vaginal capsules during fertility treatment or pregnancy:
use the capsules before bedtime
gently insert the capsule into your vagina, as deep as you can
use the capsules as often as your doctor has told you to take them
Always check the packet or leaflet that comes with your medicine. Ask a pharmacist or doctor for advice if you're not sure how to take it or have any problems taking it.
If you miss a dose of Utrogestan
If you forget to take a dose of Utrogestan, take it as soon as you remember, unless it's nearly time for the next dose.
If it's nearly time for your next dose, skip the dose you missed and take your next dose at the usual time.
Do not take 2 doses to make up for a forgotten dose.
If you take too much Utrogestan
Using too much Utrogestan is unlikely to cause problems, but it's important to get medical advice if you take too much because it can sometimes be dangerous.
It can also increase the risk of side effects such as breast tenderness, feeling sick (nausea) and vaginal bleeding.
Urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist as soon as possible if:
you've taken more than your prescribed dose of Utrogestan
If you're unable to speak to a pharmacist, call NHS 111 for advice.

Side effects of Utrogestan
Like all medicines, Utrogestan can cause side effects, although not everyone gets them.
Common side effects
Common side effects of Utrogestan oral capsules include:
feeling dizzy or tired
feeling sick (nausea)
headaches
breast pain or tenderness
Common side effects of Utrogestan vaginal capsules include:
constipation
headaches
hot flushes
stomach cramps that feel like period pain
If you're using vaginal capsules, you may also have an oily vaginal discharge, and your vagina and vulva may feel itchy.
Utrogestan will not usually affect your ability to drive, ride a bike or use machinery. However, if you feel dizzy or tired while taking it, wait until you no longer have these side effects.
Non-urgent advice: Speak to a pharmacist or doctor if:
you have any side effects that bother you or do not go away
They may be able to give you advice about how you can reduce the side effects. Keep taking your medicine unless you're advised to stop.

Serious side effects
Utrogestan oral capsules can sometimes cause serious side effects, although most of them are rare.
Possible serious side effects of Utrogestan include:
an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) – which can cause symptoms such as a swollen throat or tongue, a raised, itchy rash or difficulty breathing
stroke – which can cause symptoms such as sudden weakness on one side of your face or in one arm, being unable to hold both arms up, or problems speaking
pulmonary embolism – which can cause symptoms such as severe pain in your chest, sudden difficulty breathing or coughing up blood
a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis) – which can cause symptoms such as pain and swelling in your lower leg or foot, and the skin may be warm to touch or red (this may be harder to see on black or brown skin)
Immediate action required: Call 999 if:
you're taking Utrogestan and think you might be having anaphylaxis, a stroke or pulmonary embolism
If you think you're having any other serious side effect, call NHS 111.
There's also an increased risk of breast cancer when you take Utrogestan as part of HRT. Your doctor will talk to you about the risks and benefits.
It's important to see your GP if you notice any lumps, nipple discharge or changes in your breasts, or have other symptoms you're worried about.

Who can and cannot take Utrogestan
Most adults can take Utrogestan.
But Utrogestan is not suitable for everyone. For example, it may not be suitable if:
you've had an allergic reaction or other serious side effect from using medicines containing progesterone
you've had an allergic reaction to peanuts or soya
you've had or have breast or womb (uterus) cancer
you've had a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis)
you have a blood clotting condition
you've had a stroke, heart attack or pulmonary embolism
you have porphyria
you've had or have liver problems
you have thickening of the womb lining (endometrial hyperplasia) that's not been treated
Before taking Utrogestan, tell your doctor if you have any medical conditions.
Breastfeeding and Utrogestan
Utrogestan is not used while breastfeeding. Tell your doctor if you're breastfeeding or plan to breastfeed.
Pregnancy and Utrogestan
If you're taking Utrogestan for menopause symptoms, tell your doctor if you're pregnant or trying to get pregnant. Utrogestan is not usually used for menopause symptoms during pregnancy.
You can use Utrogestan vaginal capsules during pregnancy.
Taking Utrogestan with other medicines, food and drink
There are some medicines, remedies and supplements that may not mix well with Utrogestan.
This includes:
medicines used to treat anxiety and insomnia, such as diazepam or lorazepam
medicines used to treat high cholesterol, such as atorvastatin
medicines used to treat fungal infections, such a fluconazole
medicines used to treat diabetes
medicines used to treat seizures, such as phenobarbital or carbamazepine
medicines used to treat HIV, such as nelfinavir
some types of antibiotics, such as amoxicillin or tetracycline
bosentan, used to treat lung conditions or systemic sclerosis
bromocriptine, used to treat symptoms of Parkinson's
St John's wort
Before taking Utrogestan, tell your doctor if you're taking any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.
Food, drink and alcohol with Utrogestan
You can eat and drink as normal while taking Utrogestan.
